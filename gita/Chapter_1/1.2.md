# 1.2

**Chapter 1:** Arjuna Vishada Yoga (The Yoga of Arjuna's Despair)
**Speaker:** Sanjaya (his first words in the Gita)

> सञ्जय उवाच ।
> दृष्ट्वा तु पाण्डवानीकं व्यूढं दुर्योधनस्तदा ।
> आचार्यमुपसङ्गम्य राजा वचनमब्रवीत् ॥
>
> *sañjaya uvāca*
> *dṛṣṭvā tu pāṇḍavānīkaṁ vyūḍhaṁ duryodhanas tadā*
> *ācāryam upasaṅgamya rājā vacanam abravīt*

**Translation:** Sanjaya said: But then, having seen the army of the Pandavas drawn up in battle formation, King Duryodhana approached his teacher (Drona) and spoke these words.

## Word by word

| Word | Meaning |

| sañjayaḥ uvāca | Sanjaya said |

| tu | but, however (marks a contrast) |
| pāṇḍava-anīkam | the army of the Pandavas |
| vyūḍham | arranged in a military formation (*vyūha*) |
| duryodhanaḥ | Duryodhana |
| tadā | then, at that moment |
| ācāryam | the teacher (Drona) |
| upasaṅgamya | going up close to, approaching |
| rājā | the king |
| vacanam | words |
| abravīt | spoke |

## Context

- Dhritarashtra asked in 1.1: "What did my sons and the sons of Pandu do?" Sanjaya answers about **his son first**, because that is what the father really wants to know.
- Duryodhana's army is the larger one (eleven *akshauhinis* against the Pandavas' seven). His commander-in-chief is Bhishma. Yet he does not go to Bhishma; he goes to Drona, the teacher of both the Kauravas and the Pandavas.
- His speech to Drona fills the next verses (1.3-11). In it he points out that the Pandava army was arranged by Dhrishtadyumna, son of Drupada and Drona's own student: a pointed reminder that Drona trained the man now leading the other side.

## Essence and depth

1. **"Tu": the word that turns the story.** Dhritarashtra hoped to hear that the holy ground had made the Pandavas back down. Sanjaya's first word of reply is *tu*, "but": instead, his son saw something that shook him. Traditional commentators read the whole verse as a picture of fear behind a royal face.
2. **Seeing is where it starts.** Dhritarashtra (1.1) is blind and asks. Duryodhana can see, and what he sees, an enemy army that is well ordered, makes him anxious. Clear sight without inner steadiness does not bring peace; it brings worry. The Gita will later teach *how* to see (with equanimity, *samatvam*, 2.48).
3. **Order unsettles disorder.** *Vyūḍham* means "arranged, in formation." The smaller army is the organized one. Discipline and preparation on one side expose the restlessness on the other. Numbers did not comfort Duryodhana; the other side's order frightened him.
4. **Running to authority for reassurance.** A king goes in person (*upasaṅgamya*, "going right up close") to his teacher, skipping his own commander. It looks like respect; the speech that follows shows it is anxiety and a little manipulation: he wants Drona's loyalty confirmed. When the mind is afraid, it seeks reassurance from outside instead of steadiness from within.
5. **"Rājā": the title and the man.** Sanjaya still calls him "the king." The title is intact; the inner state is not. Outer position does not guarantee inner security.
6. **The inner reading.** If the Pandavas are the dharmic tendencies and the Kauravas the ego's tendencies, this verse shows the ego's first reaction when dharma lines up in order: alarm, then a rush to justify itself to some authority. That inner Duryodhana shows up whenever we see someone better prepared and feel threatened rather than inspired.

## In simple words

Duryodhana had the bigger army, but when he saw how well organized the other side was, he got nervous and rushed to his teacher for reassurance. Fear does not come from what we see; it comes from how unsteady we are inside when we see it.

## In daily life

- **Before a contest or interview:** seeing someone else's leaderboard rank, a friend's "solved 300 problems" post, or a long syllabus list, and feeling the Duryodhana jolt. The useful response is not to run to someone for comfort, but to return to your own *vyūha*: your plan, your next block.
- **At work and as an FDE:** walking into a client site and seeing a competitor's polished setup, or a well-prepared stakeholder. The insecure move is to go to the manager to complain or to seek validation. The steady move is to tighten your own preparation and let the work speak.
- **In relationships:** seeking constant reassurance ("are you still with me?") is often Duryodhana's walk to Drona. Security built inside does not need to be confirmed outside every hour.
- **The positive lesson:** be the Pandava army here. Order, preparation and formation are themselves a kind of strength; they unsettle chaos without a single arrow being shot.

## Sadhana (practice)

1. **Catch the "dṛṣṭvā" moment.** Once today, notice when seeing something (a message, a result, someone else's progress) creates a jolt of anxiety. Pause and name it: "I saw ___, and my mind turned it into fear." Naming it is half the steadiness.
2. **Formation before reassurance.** The next time you feel the urge to seek reassurance from someone, first write one line of your own plan (your *vyūha*: the next concrete step). Then decide whether you still need to ask.

## Reflection question

When you see someone who is better prepared than you, do you walk toward your teacher like Duryodhana, looking for reassurance, or toward your own formation, looking for the next step?
