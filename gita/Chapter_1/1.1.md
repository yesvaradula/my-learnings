# 1.1

**Chapter 1:** Arjuna Vishada Yoga (The Yoga of Arjuna's Despair)
**Speaker:** Dhritarashtra (his only verse in the Gita)

> धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे समवेता युयुत्सवः ।
> मामकाः पाण्डवाश्चैव किमकुर्वत सञ्जय ॥
>
> *dharma-kṣetre kuru-kṣetre samavetā yuyutsavaḥ*
> *māmakāḥ pāṇḍavāś caiva kim akurvata sañjaya*

**Translation:** On the field of dharma, at Kurukshetra, gathered together and eager to fight, what did my sons and the sons of Pandu do, O Sanjaya?

## Word by word

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| dharma-kṣetre | on the field of dharma (righteousness, duty) |
| kuru-kṣetre | on the field of the Kurus |
| samavetāḥ | assembled, gathered together |
| yuyutsavaḥ | desiring to fight |
| māmakāḥ | my people, "mine" |
| pāṇḍavāḥ | the sons of Pandu |
| ca eva | and also |
| kim akurvata | what did they do? |
| sañjaya | O Sanjaya |

## Context

- The blind king Dhritarashtra sits in the palace in Hastinapura. Sanjaya, granted divine sight by Vyasa, sees the battlefield and narrates it to him.
- In the Mahabharata, this question comes after Sanjaya returns with the news that Bhishma has fallen, ten days into the war. So the whole Gita is told in flashback, to a king who already fears the outcome.

## Essence and depth

1. **"Mine" and "theirs": the seed of the whole war.** Dhritarashtra raised the Pandavas after their father died, yet he says *māmakāḥ* ("mine") for his sons and *pāṇḍavāḥ* ("the Pandavas") for his nephews. One word reveals the division in his heart. The war on the field started as a war in his mind.
2. **Two kinds of blindness.** He is blind in the eyes, and also blind through attachment (*moha*) to his sons. The second blindness is the one that destroys the family. Sanjaya, by contrast, represents clear vision: he sees the truth and reports it without bias.
3. **A question that hides a fear.** The armies gathered to fight, so obviously they fought. Why ask "what did they do?" Traditional commentators read anxiety in it: he fears that the holiness of a *dharma-kṣetra* might change the outcome, that dharma itself might work against his sons. When we know we are on the wrong side, we ask questions we already know the answer to.
4. **The battlefield is a field of dharma.** The Gita does not open in a temple or a forest; it opens in the middle of a conflict. The place where the hardest decisions are made is where dharma is tested.
5. **The inner reading.** Later the Gita itself calls the body a *kṣetra*, a field (13.1). Inside each person, two sets of tendencies stand facing each other: the "mine" tendencies (selfishness, attachment, ego) and the dharma tendencies (duty, truth, compassion). Every day is a small Kurukshetra.
6. **First word and last word.** The Gita's first word is *dharma* (1.1), and its final verse (18.78) ends with *mama* ("my"). Read together: *mama dharma*, "my dharma." The whole teaching is the journey from "what is mine" to "what is my dharma."

## In simple words

A blind father asks what happened on the battlefield, and in the way he asks, he shows the real problem: he divides the world into "my people" and "the others." That one split, "mine vs. theirs," is where most conflicts start, in families, in teams and inside us.

## In daily life

- **At work:** "my team vs. their team," "my code vs. their code," "who gets the credit." The moment we think *māmakāḥ*, we stop seeing what is right and start defending what is ours. A great engineer, and especially a great Forward Deployed Engineer, asks "what is right for the client and the problem?", not "how do I protect my piece?"
- **In family and relationships:** taking sides by attachment rather than by truth, as Dhritarashtra did. Love that is blind to wrongdoing is not love; it is *moha*.
- **Inside the mind:** asking questions we already know the answer to, because we are afraid of it. Honest self-inquiry starts by noticing these.

## Sadhana (practice)

1. **Catch the "māmakāḥ" moment.** Once today, notice when you split a situation into "mine" and "theirs" (a meeting, a code review, an argument). Pause and ask: *what is the dharma here, not what is mine?*
2. **Name today's field.** In the morning, say to yourself: "Today my dharma-kshetra is ___" (the class, the task, the conversation that will test you). Walk into it knowing it is a field of dharma, not just a chore.

## Reflection question

Where in your life right now are you being a Dhritarashtra: seeing something clearly enough to worry about it, but too attached to look at it honestly?
